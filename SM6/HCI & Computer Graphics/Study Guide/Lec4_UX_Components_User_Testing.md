## Part 1: Methods of UX Research

### Three Foundational Methods of UX Research

**ASK**
Understand users' perspectives by asking them directly about their thoughts, needs, and behaviors.

**OBSERVE**
Gain insights by watching users interact with a system or perform tasks in their natural environment.

**INSPECT**
Evaluate designs and prototypes against established guidelines and best practices to identify potential usability issues.

---

### Comprehensive UX Research Framework

The framework spans three phases of the product development lifecycle:

**Assess**
- Interviews, Surveys, Comparative Analysis

**Design**
- Lo-Fi Testing, Hi-Fi Testing

**Build**
- Heuristic Evaluation, Analytics, Observations

A multi-faceted approach combines various techniques across the product development lifecycle to ensure a holistic understanding of user needs and system performance.

---

### Ask: Gathering User Perspectives

**Interviews**
Structured conversations with stakeholders to deeply understand their experiences, motivations, and challenges.

**Surveys**
Distributed questionnaires to a large audience to collect quantitative and qualitative data on attitudes, behaviors, and demographics.

**Focus Groups**
Facilitated discussions with small groups of users to explore specific topics and gather collective opinions.

**Diary Studies**
Participants record their experiences, thoughts, and activities over an extended period to capture real-time usage and context.

**Experience Sampling**
Collecting data on users' experiences at random intervals in their natural environment, often via mobile devices.

---

### Observe: Understanding User Behavior

**Ethnographic Observations**
Watching users in their natural environment to understand how they interact with products and perform tasks without intervention.

**User Testing**
Observing users as they complete specific, scripted tasks within a system to identify usability issues and assess task completion rates.

**Usage Analytics**
Analyzing large datasets of user interactions (e.g., clicks, navigation paths) to uncover patterns and trends in system usage.

**Video Analysis**
Reviewing recorded user sessions to meticulously analyze interactions, gestures, and expressions, providing rich qualitative data.

**Social Media Mining**
Extracting and analyzing publicly available data from social media to understand user sentiment, feedback, and trends related to a product.

---

### Inspect: Evaluating Designs

**Guideline-Based**
Systematic evaluation of a design against established usability heuristics, accessibility standards, or industry best practices.

**Walkthroughs**
Simulating user interaction paths step-by-step to uncover potential friction points or breakdowns in the user journey.

**Comparative Analysis**
Assessing a product's design by comparing it against competitors or similar solutions to pinpoint strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities.

---

### Hybrid Approaches: Watch and Ask

Often, combining observation with direct questioning yields richer insights, capturing both explicit and implicit user behaviors and motivations.

- **User Testing + Interviews** - Observing users perform tasks, then asking follow-up questions to understand their decision-making and rationale.
- **Contextual Interviews** - Interviewing users in their natural environment while they are engaging in relevant activities, capturing insights in real-time context.
- **Artifact-based Methods** - Analyzing existing user-generated artifacts (e.g., notes, sketches, logs) to understand workflows and challenges, often combined with interviews about these artifacts.

---

### When to Apply Which Method?

**Ask when:**
- Observation is infeasible due to infrequent, long-duration, or private activities.
- Understanding user values, motivations, and emotional responses is key.
- Large numbers and high certainty are needed (e.g., through surveys).

**Observe when:**
- Understanding actual behavior, not just reported behavior, is crucial.
- Identifying usability issues in context is important.
- Discovering unforeseen usage patterns is desired.

**Inspect when:**
- There is a need for a quick and cost-effective expert evaluation.
- Benchmarking against established standards or competitors is required.
- Identifying probable flaws before extensive user testing is beneficial.

## Part 2: Components of UX

### Good and Bad UX

Good UX qualities include: Useful, Helpful, Accessible, Connected, Attractive, Fun, Satisfying, Easy to Learn, Delightful.

Bad UX qualities include: Stressful, Ugly, Frustrating, Confusing, Distracting, Condescending, Tedious, Inefficient, Inconsiderate.

---

### The Four Components of UX

| Component | Key Question (Understanding Users) | Key Question (Evaluating Designs) |
|---|---|---|
| **Value** | What do users need? | Does this design fulfill the need? |
| **Usability** | How do they do it now? | Can they get it done with this? |
| **Desirability** | What do they desire? | Is the design appealing? |
| **Adoptability** | Where do users look for things? | Can users find and access this? |

**Value** - Is it useful? Is it better than the alternatives?

**Usability** - Can users do what they need to do?

**Adoptability** - Is it easy to find and start using?

**Desirability** - Is it fun, attractive, and pleasant to use?

---

### Real-World Examples

**VisiCalc (1979)** - An early spreadsheet application demonstrating functional value even without aesthetic desirability.

**Motorola ROKR** - An example of a product that failed to achieve strong adoptability and desirability despite having some functional value.

**iPod (2001)** - An example of a product that succeeded across all four UX components: valuable, usable, adoptable, and desirable.

**Duolingo Onboarding Process** - A strong example of adoptability; the onboarding is simple, frictionless, and gets users started immediately with "Learn a language for free. Forever."

---

### Basic Methods of UX

The three basic methods map directly onto the four UX components:

- Understanding Users
- Design and Prototyping
- Evaluating Designs

---

### The Iterative UX Process

The UX process is cyclical and continuous:

**Assess -> Design -> Build -> (repeat)**

Each phase feeds back into the others and is evaluated against the four components of Value, Usability, Adoptability, and Desirability.

---

### The 7 Factors that Influence User Experience

(Based on Peter Morville's work)

1. Useful
2. Usable
3. Findable
4. Credible
5. Accessible
6. Desirable
7. Valuable

Reference: https://www.interactiondesign.org/literature/article/the-7-factors-that-influence-user-experience

---

### The UX Honeycomb (Peter Morville)

The UX Honeycomb places **Valuable** at the center, surrounded by six facets:

- **Useful** - Does it serve a purpose?
- **Usable** - Is it easy and efficient to use?
- **Desirable** - Is it aesthetically and emotionally engaging?
- **Findable** - Can users navigate and locate what they need?
- **Credible** - Do users trust it?
- **Accessible** - Can users of all abilities use it?

All six outer facets contribute to making a product Valuable at the core.

Reference: http://semanticstudios.com/user_experience_design/

## Part 3: User Testing (Micro-Usability Testing)

### What is User Testing?

User testing (also called usability testing) involves watching representative users try to accomplish important tasks using a product.

- **Representative Users** - the types of people that you expect to use a system.
- Also known as Usability Testing.
- Note: there is more to UX than just usability testing.

---

### Why User Testing?

You learn a large amount from watching people use a system:
- What works and what does not work.
- Why things work and do not work.
- User needs that you missed.

**Why not just use your own experience?**
- You often know too much (expert bias).
- You might know too little (unfamiliarity with users' context).

---

### Basic Idea of User Testing

1. Plan the Usability Test
2. Find Potential Users
3. Ask them to do some stuff (Tasks)
4. Observe - Use Think Aloud Protocol
5. Ask some questions - Debrief
6. Write down what you learnt

---

### Potential Users

Participants should be people who fall within the target audience, defined by:
- Attitudes
- Behaviours
- Characteristics

They should generally not be current users of the exact system being tested. However, it is acceptable if they are current users of the system but have not previously performed the selected tasks.

---

### Tasks: What You Ask Them to Do

Tasks are the scenarios or actions you ask participants to complete. Examples:
- "Buy a wireless presenter that costs less than $50 and has a rechargeable battery."
- "Enter a review for the most recent book you read."
- "Buy a hardbound book by a Nigerian author that was published this year."

---

### Choosing Tasks

Tasks should cover:
- Things that **most** users need to do (e.g., "Buy a Book!")
- More difficult things that **some** users need to do (e.g., "Write a Review")
- Complex, specific scenarios (e.g., "Buy a hardbound book by a Nigerian author that was published this year.")

---

### Task Types

**Closed-ended tasks**
- Have a clear end point.
- Have a verifiable outcome.
- Follow an expected path.
- Example: "Buy a hardbound book by a Nigerian author that was published this year."

**Open-ended tasks**
- Allow the user to judge when complete.
- May not be verifiable.
- Allow following alternate paths.
- Example: "Find some books you might like to read on vacation!"

---

### Closed-ended vs Open-ended: Which are better?

| Closed-ended | Open-ended |
|---|---|
| Less natural | More natural |
| Controls for motivation | Varying motivation |
| Controls for interpretation | Varying interpretation |
| Easier to assess success | Success is ambiguous |

Neither is universally better. The choice depends on your research goals.

---

### Task Sets

- Progress from easier to harder tasks (builds confidence, avoids early abandonment).
- Cover a range of critical task types (e.g., browse, search, buy).
- Can include both open-ended and closed-ended tasks.
- Be careful to avoid "ordering effects" - where the order of tasks influences performance on subsequent tasks.

Reference: Better Experimental Design for Better User Testing: http://www.uxbooth.com/articles/better-experimental-design-for-better-user-testing/

## Part 4: Task Design - Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Leading the Witness

Do not use wording that reveals the expected path or solution.

**Bad example:**
"Put 3 books in your shopping cart, then purchase them using Standard Shipping."
(This tells the user to use the shopping cart and Standard Shipping, which are UI-specific terms.)

**Better example:**
"Choose three books and buy them, making sure they can get here by next Wednesday."

---

### Pitfall 2: Ambiguity

Avoid vague or ambiguous task wording.

**Bad example:**
"Use a list to find a gift for your ten year old nephew."
(What list? The word "list" is ambiguous.)

**Better example:**
"A friend told you that Amazon has helpful gift idea lists for different age groups. Find a list that would be useful for finding a gift for your 10-year old nephew's birthday party, and select a gift for him."

---

### Pitfall 3: Lack of Context and Motivation

Tasks without context feel unnatural. Users need a reason to care.

**Bad example:**
"Buy a book and have it shipped by Monday!"

**Better example:**
"It's Friday, and you've just realized that you need a book on Japanese business etiquette by Monday, so that you have time to brush up before your meeting with colleagues from Japan on Wednesday. Find and purchase an appropriate book, making sure it arrives in time."

---

### Pitfall 4: Not Telling Users When to Stop

Always include a stopping instruction.

**Good example (complete task with stopping instruction):**
"It's Friday, and you've just realized that you need a book on Japanese business etiquette by Monday, so that you have time to brush up before your meeting with colleagues from Japan on Wednesday. Find and purchase an appropriate book, making sure it arrives in time.

When you feel you have completed the task, please say 'I'm done.'"

---

### Pitfall 5: Not Pilot Testing

Task wording is very hard to get right on the first try. Always pilot test:
- Try tasks yourself first.
- Try them with a friend or two.
- Then run them with real participants.

Pilot testing catches ambiguities, misleading wording, and timing issues before the actual test.

## Part 5: Think Aloud Protocol

### What is Think Aloud?

Participants verbalize (say out loud) what they are thinking as they interact with the system. This includes:
- What they are looking for
- Text they are reading
- Hypotheses about how the system might work
- Interpretation of system options
- Interpretation of system feedback
- Explanation of decisions
- Emotional reactions (frustration, happiness, confusion)

---

### Managing Think Aloud

Think aloud is not natural for most people. Participants may go quiet when absorbed in a task.

Gently remind them with prompts such as:
- "Could you let me know what you're doing and what you're thinking?"
- "Make sure you keep telling me what's going on, so that I can understand how you're going about this process."

---

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Think Aloud

**Advantages:**
- You hear how the user thinks about the task.
- You learn what the user actually sees and notices.
- You hear how the user interprets options and feedback.

**Disadvantages:**
- Timing will not be realistic (verbalizing slows users down).
- Attention to detail will not be quite realistic.
- You need to determine rules of engagement for when users ask questions or make mistakes.

**Recommendation:** For problem finding purposes, the advantages outweigh the disadvantages. Use Think Aloud.

---

## Part 6: Debrief (After Tasks)

The debrief happens after all tasks are complete. It is a structured conversation to gather additional insight.

**Review problems and get more information.**

**Ask about usefulness and value:**
- What would you find it useful for?
- How would it fit into your life or workflow?
- How would you value this?
- Where would this provide a real benefit for you, or would it provide a benefit at all?

**Ask about perceived usability:**
- Did they feel like it was usable?
- Note: perceived usability can differ from observed usability.

**Ask about other UX dimensions:**
- Aesthetics and visual appeal
- Credibility and trustworthiness
- Accessibility

**Compare to current alternatives:**
- What do they currently use for this?
- How does this compare?

## Part 7: Making Sense of the Test

### Capturing Critical Incidents

A critical incident is anything worth noting that occurred during the test:
- **Errors** - Users did not do what was expected, or did not follow the expected path for accomplishing a task.
- **Expressed frustration** - Visible or verbal signs of frustration.
- **Breakdowns** - Points where the user completely lost their way or gave up.
- **Pleasant surprises** - Moments where users reacted positively in unexpected ways.

---

### Assessing Overall Success and Failure

Success and failure are usually a spectrum, not binary. Consider:
- Did the user complete the task?
- How much help or prompting did they need?
- How long did it take relative to the expected time?

---

### Capturing Overall and Specific Reactions

- What was the participant's overall demeanor - positive, upbeat, deflated, frustrated, bored?
- What were the causes of those different reactions?
- Link specific incidents to subjective reactions.
  - Did frustration result from a particular design element?
  - Were there specific incidents that led to a positive reaction?

---

## Part 8: Learning from the Test

### Writing It Down

Write down findings immediately after the session while memory is fresh. Document:
- Critical incidents and why they happened
- Mental model mismatches (user expected a different behavior)
- Misinterpretations of labels, options, or feedback
- Invalid assumptions made by the system about the user
- Missing user needs not addressed by the design
- Too little flexibility (system forces one path)
- Too little guidance (system does not help users when they are lost)

---

### Assessing Problem Severity

For each problem identified, assess its severity based on its impact on:
- **Success/Failure** - Did this problem prevent task completion?
- **Subjective experience** - Did this problem negatively affect how users felt about the system?
- **Product goals** - Did this problem undermine what the product is trying to achieve?

**Other UX factors to consider:**
- Usefulness
- Credibility
- Accessibility

---

## Part 9: Ethics in User Testing

### Important Ethical Considerations

- Participation is voluntary. No participant should feel coerced.
- Participants can stop the session at any time.
- You are testing the system, not the participant. Participants should never feel judged or evaluated for their performance.
- You must clearly communicate all of the above to participants before starting.

---

## Part 10: What Makes a Micro-Usability Test "Micro"?

A micro-usability test is a lightweight, informal version of a standard usability test.

**Relaxed recruiting:**
- Participants are people close enough to the target audience that they can reasonably imagine being in that user role.
- Also known as a "hallway" usability test.

**Fewer tasks:**
- Duration is under 30 minutes, compared to 60-90 minutes for a standard usability test.

**Little or no formal data collection:**
- No recording.
- No questionnaires.
- No interaction logging.

**Off-the-cuff analysis:**
- Observations and insights are captured informally and immediately after the session.
