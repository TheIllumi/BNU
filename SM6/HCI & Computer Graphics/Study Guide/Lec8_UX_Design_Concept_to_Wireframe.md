## Wicked Problems

Wicked problems in design:

- Are ill-defined / underspecified
- Do not have a right or wrong solution
- Are context-dependent (and, so, unique)
- Do not have a clear test for solutions

## What is Design?

### Steve Jobs
> "Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it looks like. People think it's this veneer - that the designers are handed this box and told, 'Make it look good!' That's not what we think design is. It's not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."
> - Steve Jobs

### Charles Eames
> "[Design is] a plan for arranging elements in such a way as to best accomplish a particular purpose."
> - Charles Eames

### Christopher Alexander
> "...every design problem begins with an effort to achieve fitness between two entities: the form in question and its context."
> - Christopher Alexander

### Bill Buxton
> "Design is Compromise."
> - Bill Buxton

### Core Definition

**Purpose:** What is the function of this object?

**Context:** In what situations does this thing need to be used?

**Constraints:** What determines how this thing can be made and used?

**Design** is about creating things that fulfill their **purpose** well, given the **constraints**.

---

## Core Design Skills

1. To frame, or reframe, the problem and the objective
2. To create and envision alternatives
3. To select from those alternatives
4. To visualize and prototype the intended solution
5. To synthesize a solution that addresses all important constraints

---

## Design Is a Process

Design follows a structured process moving through three phases: **Define**, **Frame**, and **Implement**.

- **Strategy (Define):** Goals, Business Goals, User Needs
- **Tactics (Frame):** Opportunities, Content Functions, Structure Interface
- **Implementation (Implement):** Integration Plan, Visual Design, Front End Code

### Design Process (In a Nutshell)

**Framing the problem:**
- User research
- Competitive analysis
- Data analysis and summary

**Exploring the solution space:**
- Brainstorming
- Sketching

**Finding a good solution:**
- Scoping
- Consideration of constraints
- Scenarios, storyboards, personas
- Design rationale

**Refining the solution:**
- Wireframes
- Lo-fi prototypes
- Early evaluations
- Mockups/mid-fi prototypes
- Additional evaluations

## Framing the Design Problem

### Goal

Formulate the problem and understand important constraints a solution needs to satisfy.

### Frame

A frame is an active perspective that both describes and perceptually changes a given situation.

### What's In and What's Out

Frame describes aspects of the design problem the designer considers to be important and will focus on.

### Scope

How "big" is the problem you are focusing on?

### Design Space

What can vary in how you are thinking about the problem?

### Framing the Right Design Problem

No design problem exists fully formed. A key job of a designer is to specify the exact problem that needs to be solved.

### Framing is an Iterative Process

The frame changes because:
- Project timeline changes
- Budget gets cut
- Designer's understanding evolves

## Personas: Concretizing User Needs

### You Are NOT Your User

Designers must resist the temptation to design for themselves. The intended users are different people with different needs, goals, contexts, and constraints.

### What Are Personas?

Personas are fictional characters that represent key types of technology's intended users that were discovered through formative work.

### Key Characteristics of Personas

- Based in data
- Embody the various goals, traits, behaviors, and contexts that could influence if and how the technology is going to be used
- Represent distinct classes of intended users

### Sample Persona: Mary

**Demographics:**
- Age: 49
- Occupation: Project manager
- Location: Irvine, CA
- Life stage: Divorced, one child

**Motivators:**
- Professional success
- Being a good mom to her 10-year-old daughter
- Novelty: Mary gets bored easily and has a high need for novel experiences
- Social contact: Mary is very social and enjoys working with people and spending social time with friends

**Constraints:**
- Suburban living environment: Mary gets little activity in routine daily life
- Work days packed with meetings
- Free evening time limited: Mary often works in the evening, or wants to spend time with her daughter. Free on nights when daughter is with her dad
- High technical literacy

### Sample Persona: John

**Demographics:**
- Age: 68
- Occupation: Mechanical engineer
- Location: Ann Arbor, MI
- Life stage: Retired, living with his wife

**Motivators:**
- Staying healthy as long as possible
- Spending quality time with his grandchildren
- Being independent and not being a burden on his children
- Continuing to learn

**Constraints:**
- Weather: Often too cold or too hot to exercise outdoors
- Access to stationary bike at home
- Strong need for structure and routine
- Travels to spend time with grandchildren
- Moderate technical literacy

### How to Create Personas

Reference: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B23iWg0koi8

### Elements of Personas

**Demographics:**
- Age, technical literacy
- Kind of living environment (if relevant)
- Potential socioeconomic status
- Issues that might affect how they would be using the technology

**Goals and Motivators:**
- Issues related to how the technology would be used
- The goals that these individuals will be pursuing using the technology
- Motivators that could be leveraged to help them use the technology, or that might act as barriers to technology use

**Behaviors:**
- Routines and work practices are often included insofar as they are things to which technology can be integrated
- Or if they might act as barriers to technology use

**Context:**
- Living and working environment
- Other aspects of physical and social context that might be relevant to determining technology use

### Benefits of Personas

- Personalize and summarize a lot of data from the formative research
- Provide the mental shortcut for design considerations ("Would Mary do this?" / "How would John react to this particular feature?")
- Allow discussion of and agreement on who the intended users are
- Easy to understand by non-designers
- Help check tendencies to design for oneself or the "elastic user"

### Some Final Considerations for Personas

- Personas should feel real. They should accurately summarize available data.
- Personas should always try to focus on common rather than idiosyncratic characteristics.
- Content should be relevant (e.g. family situation might not be relevant for design of a workplace technology)

## Design Scenarios

### What Are Scenarios?

Scenarios are short stories about specific users who are using technology being designed to accomplish specific goals in a specific context.

Scenarios bridge findings about user needs and ideas about how a technology may support those needs.

### Sample Scenario (HealthyHeart App - Mary)

Ever since Mary returned to work after her heart attack last year, she has struggled to incorporate regular physical activity into her daily life. HealthyHeart, an app she recently discovered, is slowly changing that. As Mary is finishing her meeting, her activity tracker gently vibrates to indicate that HealthyHeart has sent her an activity suggestion. When Mary looks at her phone, she finds a message showing her a 1500-step walking route to her next meeting that goes by the beautiful fountain on her company's campus. She has just enough time to walk over, so she hits "thumbs-up" to indicate she liked the suggestion, and she follows the suggested route. That evening, after dinner, HealthyHeart alerts her that there is a salsa dance three days later at a restaurant 20 minutes away. "Oh, great!" Mary thinks. "This could be a fun way to get more intense activity to meet my weekly activity prescription." She taps "let's do it!" and salsa dancing, along with the directions, is added to her calendar.

### What Did We Learn From This Scenario?

- Mary's days are packed with meetings
- HealthyHeart is proactive
- Its suggestions are just-in-time and contextually tailored
- It helps Mary discover novel physical activities
- It is intended to induce a feeling of pleasant surprise

### Why Use Scenarios?

- Support reflection during the design process
- Open-ended and easily revised
- Anchor design discussion

### Some HealthyHeart Considerations (Raised by the Scenario)

- How many suggestions would Mary tolerate before getting annoyed?
- What would the system need to know to provide good suggestions?
- How should such a system respond if Mary does not like a suggestion?
- Where would ideas for novel activities come from?

### Elements of a Scenario

- **Setting:** Situation (state of the system, context of use) in which activity occurs
- **Agent(s):** People performing the activity described in scenario
- **Goals:** Objectives agents are trying to accomplish
- **Actions:** Activities that agents do to accomplish their goals
- **Events:** Things that happen to users while doing the activities in the scenario

### Types of Scenarios

**Problem scenarios:**
- Describe features of the current situation

**Goal or task-based scenarios:**
- What the user wants to do

**Activity scenarios:**
- Transform current practice into new design features

**Full scale task scenarios:**
- Steps to accomplish a task

**Information scenarios:**
- How users perceive, interpret, and make sense of information

**Interaction scenarios:**
- Physical actions and system responses that respond to users' tasks

### Scenario Considerations

- You do not need to be a great writer to write useful scenarios
- Scenarios should be quick. More rough scenarios are often more useful than fewer scenarios that are more refined
- Scenarios are process artifacts. Do only as much as is helpful
- Scenarios can help uncover unwanted technology effects

### Scenario Summary

- Can help test early design ideas
- Support communication
- Help designers reflect on intended and unintended consequences of their design decisions

## Storyboards

### What Is a Storyboard?

A storyboard is a series of sketches that visually conveys how a user engages in an activity that involves the technology that is being developed.

Storyboards are visual presentations of stories about how individuals use the technology that is being developed to support their goals.

### Lessons Learnt From Storyboarding (HealthyHeart Example)

- If suggestions are not doable, it leads to user frustration
- If just-in-time suggestions are poorly timed, it can be really annoying
- Frustration accumulates. Enough of it, and the system might be abandoned

### Why Use Storyboards (If You Already Have Scenarios)?

They help you think more deeply about:
- Environments where the system is used
- Physical constraints (size of system, space where it is used)
- User's motivation and emotion
- Relationships among multiple people

### Elements of a Storyboard

- **Zoom:** How wide/narrow is the focus of the frame?
- **Angle:** From whose point of view is the content in the frame? User's? Observer's?
- **Detail:** What is the frame focused on?
- **Emotion:** What emotion is the user expressing?
- **Setting:** Where does the action take place?

### Storyboard Considerations

- How many panels are needed?
- What interactions will be represented?
- How will time be represented?
- How will the user ("character") fit?
- What text will be used?
- Outline screens
- Add detail as needed

### Some Final Thoughts on Storyboards

- You do not need to be a great artist to draw useful storyboards
- Storyboarding quickly helps you create more storyboards
- Storyboards are most helpful for conveying physical environment, emotion, and relationships
- Play to your strengths: often either a scenario or a storyboard will work

## Design Rationale: Making Design Decisions

### The Challenge of Making Choices

Designers constantly face a large number of possible design options. The job is not merely to pick one, but to make principled, reasoned decisions about which design option to pursue.

### What Is Design Rationale?

Design rationale is the articulation and analysis of tradeoffs of different alternatives to guide design.

**Goals:** To understand the design options, their pros and cons, and to make principled decisions about which design options to pursue.

---

## Questions, Options, Criteria (QOC Framework)

> MacLean, A., Young, R.M, Bellotti, V. & Moran, T.P. (1991). Questions, options, and criteria: Elements of design space analysis. *Human-Computer Interaction* 6, 3-4: 201-250.

- A feature is represented by multiple possible **options** which answer a particular **question**
- **Criteria** help articulate their tradeoffs and guide the choice of which option(s) to go with

---

## QOC Applied: Diet Tracking Application

### Questions

Questions:
- Provide structure to the design space
- Help uncover and define alternatives

**Example questions for a diet tracking app:**
- How should consumed food be entered into the food log?
- What kinds of food should be tracked?
- At what granularity should food be tracked?

### Options

Options are different potential design answers to the same question.

**For the question "How should consumed food be entered into the food log?":**
- By finding consumed food in a database
- By logging its nutritional content (grains, fruits, etc.)
- By taking a picture of food
- (other options)

### Criteria

Criteria are required and desirable properties the design should satisfy. They:
- Differ in importance and generality
- Help determine reasons for decisions

#### Sources of Criteria

- Formative work with target users
- Usability broadly construed (e.g. integration with daily routines, privacy, social acceptability, user expectations, etc.)
- Previous studies
- Behavioral theory
- Requirements from other parts of the design

#### Food Tracking Criteria (Example)

- Can be maintained long-term
- Does not require deep nutritional knowledge
- Fast to use
- Easy to use from the beginning
- Supports graphing and statistical analysis of data

### Evaluating Options Using Criteria

Each design option is evaluated against the criteria. Options that satisfy more of the most important criteria are favored. After evaluation, options that best meet the criteria become contenders for narrowing design focus.

### Things to Consider When Using QOC

- There might not be one clear answer
- It is important to know the most important criteria
- A design decision can affect many other aspects of the project
- Empirical data (from surveys, focus groups, small studies) can inform design decisions

### Summary of Design Rationale and QOC

- Deciding which option to pursue is a key design activity
- Design decisions need to be intentional and reasoned
- QOC enables designers to make design decisions in a systematic way
