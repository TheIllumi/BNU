## Lecture 3: User-Centered Design

---

### The Essence of User-Centered Design

User-Centered Design (UCD) is an iterative design process in which designers focus on the users and their needs in each phase of the design process. UCD calls for involving users throughout the design process via a variety of research and design techniques so as to create highly usable and accessible products for them.

---

### UX Process Overview

The UX process is a cyclical workflow that moves through the following stages:

- **Strategy** - Setting overall direction
- **Research** - Interviews, observation, surveys
- **Analysis** - Personas, scenarios, synthesis
- **Design** - Workflow, wireframes, prototypes
- **Production** - Visual design, development, technical testing
- **Evaluate** - Testing and evaluation loop
- **Beta Launch** - Pre-release testing
- **Launch** - Final product release

---

### UX Process - Phase 1: Strategy & Research

#### Strategy: Setting the Course

- Articulates the brand's guiding principles and long-term vision.
- Shapes project goals: what to achieve, how to measure success, and its priority.
- Defines the foundational purpose and direction for the entire project.

#### Research: Uncovering Insights

Also known as the Discovery phase, this involves:

- Gathering user and competitor data through various methods (interviews, surveys, observations).
- Crucial for creating an informed user experience.
- Often undervalued or skipped in "Lean UX" approaches, despite its importance.

---

### UX Process - Phase 2: Analysis & Design

#### Analysis: Interpreting Data

- Draws actionable insights from data collected during the research phase.
- Organizes and infers meaning from observations to understand the "why."
- Communicating findings back to end-users validates assumptions.

#### Design: Crafting Solutions

- A collaborative and iterative process.
- Involves putting ideas in front of users, gathering feedback, and refining concepts.
- Utilizes low-fidelity prototypes (paper, wireframes) to focus on functionality over aesthetics.

---

### UX Process - Phase 3: Production

The production phase brings the design to life with meticulous attention to detail:

- High-fidelity designs are fully fleshed out, incorporating all visual elements and branding.
- Content is finalized and digital assets are created or integrated.
- The near-final product undergoes rigorous user testing and validation with stakeholders and end-users to ensure it meets all requirements and delivers an optimal experience.

---

### Rapid Prototyping / Iterative Design

Iterative design follows a cycle of repeating stages to continuously improve the product:

1. **Initial Planning**
2. **Requirements**
3. **Analysis & Design**
4. **Implementation**
5. **Deployment**
6. **Testing**
7. **Evaluation**

This cycle repeats until the product meets quality standards.

Reference: https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/article/design-iteration-brings-powerful-results-so-do-it-again-designer

---

### Lean UX

The Lean UX process framework operates on a simple three-step loop:

1. **Think** - Formulate hypotheses and ideas
2. **Make** - Build something to test the idea
3. **Check** - Validate against user needs

---

### Iterative Design (Core Loop)

The iterative design cycle consists of three phases:

- **Assess / Research**
- **Design**
- **Build**

#### Assess Phase

- Understand what users are currently doing
- Identify what their needs are
- Understand the design space
- Identify the problems that they are facing

#### Design Phase

- Take what was learned in the initial assessment
- Generate ideas - lots of ideas

#### Build Phase

- Take those design ideas (or a subset)
- Build them into prototypes or some kind of representation that can be used to communicate a particular idea
- Get feedback on whether that idea is headed in the right direction

#### (Re)-Assess Phase

- Re-apply assessment methods, like user testing or formal inspection methods, to see if the prototype is leading toward a good user experience or a bad one
- Take what is learned and repeat the entire cycle again
- Go back to the drawing board
- Design new ideas
- Build new prototypes
- Perform new assessments
- Continue this until the product is ready to go

---

### Integrate Research and Design

The iterative loop maps directly to:

- **Assess** phase uses **UX Research Methods**
- **Design** phase uses **UX Design Methods**

#### UX Research: Key Methods

- Interviews
- Observations
- Surveys
- User Testing
- Inspection Methods

#### UX Design: Key Methods

- Personas, Scenarios, User Stories
- Sketching and Ideation
- Storyboarding
- Mapping and Navigation Design
- Comparative Research
- Lo-, Mid-, and Hi-Fidelity Prototyping

## Lecture 4: UX Components and User Testing

---

### Methods of UX Research

---

### Three Foundational Methods of UX Research

| Method | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Ask** | Understand users' perspectives by asking them directly about their thoughts, needs, and behaviors. |
| **Observe** | Gain insights by watching users interact with a system or perform tasks in their natural environment. |
| **Inspect** | Evaluate designs and prototypes against established guidelines and best practices to identify potential usability issues. |

---

### Comprehensive UX Research Framework

A multi-faceted approach combines various techniques across the product development lifecycle to ensure a holistic understanding of user needs and system performance.

| Phase | Techniques |
|-------|-----------|
| **Assess** | Interviews, Surveys, Comparative Analysis |
| **Design** | Lo-Fi Testing, Hi-Fi Testing |
| **Build** | Heuristic Evaluation, Analytics, Observations |

---

### Ask: Gathering User Perspectives

**Interviews**
Structured conversations with stakeholders to deeply understand their experiences, motivations, and challenges.

**Surveys**
Distributed questionnaires to a large audience to collect quantitative and qualitative data on attitudes, behaviors, and demographics.

**Focus Groups**
Facilitated discussions with small groups of users to explore specific topics and gather collective opinions.

**Diary Studies**
Participants record their experiences, thoughts, and activities over an extended period to capture real-time usage and context.

**Experience Sampling**
Collecting data on users' experiences at random intervals in their natural environment, often via mobile devices.

---

### Observe: Understanding User Behavior

**Ethnographic Observations**
Watching users in their natural environment to understand how they interact with products and perform tasks without intervention.

**User Testing**
Observing users as they complete specific, scripted tasks within a system to identify usability issues and assess task completion rates.

**Usage Analytics**
Analyzing large datasets of user interactions (e.g., clicks, navigation paths) to uncover patterns and trends in system usage.

**Video Analysis**
Reviewing recorded user sessions to meticulously analyze interactions, gestures, and expressions, providing rich qualitative data.

**Social Media Mining**
Extracting and analyzing publicly available data from social media to understand user sentiment, feedback, and trends related to a product.

---

### Inspect: Evaluating Designs

**Guideline-Based**
Systematic evaluation of a design against established usability heuristics, accessibility standards, or industry best practices.

**Walkthroughs**
Simulating user interaction paths step-by-step to uncover potential friction points or breakdowns in the user journey.

**Comparative Analysis**
Assessing a product's design by comparing it against competitors or similar solutions to pinpoint strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities.

---

### Hybrid Approaches: Watch and Ask

Often, combining observation with direct questioning yields richer insights, capturing both explicit and implicit user behaviors and motivations.

**User Testing + Interviews**
Observing users perform tasks, then asking follow-up questions to understand their decision-making and rationale.

**Contextual Interviews**
Interviewing users in their natural environment while they are engaging in relevant activities, capturing insights in real-time context.

**Artifact-based Methods**
Analyzing existing user-generated artifacts (e.g., notes, sketches, logs) to understand workflows and challenges, often combined with interviews about these artifacts.

---

### When to Apply Which Method?

**Ask when...**
- Observation is infeasible due to infrequent, long-duration, or private activities.
- Understanding user values, motivations, and emotional responses is key.
- Large numbers and high certainty are needed (e.g., through surveys).

**Observe when...**
- Understanding actual behavior, not just reported behavior, is crucial.
- Identifying usability issues in context is important.
- Discovering unforeseen usage patterns is desired.

**Inspect when...**
- There is a need for a quick and cost-effective expert evaluation.
- Benchmarking against established standards or competitors is required.
- Identifying probable flaws before extensive user testing is beneficial.

### Good and Bad UX

**Good UX qualities:** Useful, Helpful, Accessible, Connected, Attractive, Fun, Satisfying, Easy to Learn, Delightful

**Bad UX qualities:** Stressful, Ugly, Frustrating, Confusing, Distracting, Condescending, Tedious, Inefficient, Inconsiderate

---

### The Four Components of UX

| Component | Question (Understanding Users) | Question (Evaluating Designs) |
|-----------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Value** | What do users need? | Does this design fulfill the need? |
| **Usability** | How do they do it now? | Can they get it done with this? |
| **Desirability** | What do they desire? | Is the design appealing? |
| **Adoptability** | Where do users look for things? | Can users find and access this? |

#### Value
- Is it useful?
- Is it better than the alternatives?

#### Usability
- Can users do what they need to do?

#### Adoptability
- Is it easy to find and start using?

#### Desirability
- Is it fun, attractive, and pleasant to use?

---

### Examples Illustrating UX Components

**VisiCalc (1979)** - An early spreadsheet program demonstrating value through functional utility despite a minimal visual interface.

**Motorola ROKR** - An example of a product that had value (music on a phone) but fell short on usability and desirability.

**iPod (2001)** - Succeeded across all four UX components: valuable, usable, desirable, and adoptable.

**Duolingo Onboarding Process** - A strong example of adoptability: simple, clear entry point that makes it easy for new users to find and start using the product immediately.

---

### Basic Methods of UX (Mapped to Components)

Three basic methods are used in UX work, each mapping to the four UX components:

1. **Understanding Users** - informs Value, Usability, Desirability, Adoptability
2. **Design & Prototyping** - shapes solutions for each component
3. **Evaluating Designs** - validates each component

---

### The Process is your Guide

The iterative Assess-Design-Build cycle directly drives the four UX components:

- **Assess** - understand users across Value, Usability, Desirability, Adoptability
- **Design** - craft solutions targeting each component
- **Build** - implement and then re-assess

---

### The 7 Factors that Influence User Experience

Based on Peter Morville's UX Honeycomb model, the seven factors are:

1. **Useful** - Does it serve a purpose?
2. **Usable** - Is it easy to use?
3. **Desirable** - Is it visually and emotionally appealing?
4. **Findable** - Can users locate it and navigate within it?
5. **Accessible** - Can people with disabilities use it?
6. **Credible** - Do users trust it?
7. **Valuable** - Does it deliver value to the business and the user?

Reference: https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/article/the-7-factors-that-influence-user-experience

---

### The UX Honeycomb (Peter Morville)

The UX Honeycomb places **Valuable** at the center, surrounded by:

- Useful
- Usable
- Desirable
- Findable
- Credible
- Accessible

Each facet must be addressed to deliver a complete and meaningful user experience.
