## Lec 7: Design, Prototyping and Sketching

## 1. The Design Process

The design process is a cyclic, iterative sequence of stages:

1. **Understand the Problem**
2. **Generate Possible Solutions**
3. **Analyze and Select**
4. **Embody Solutions**
5. **Assess (Find New Problems)**
6. **...and Repeat**

---

## 2. Aligning The Design Process

The design process maps onto a three-phase cycle:

- **Assess** → **Design** → **Build** → (repeat)

This circular model emphasizes that design is never truly finished; each build phase leads back to assessment, which feeds back into the design phase.

---

## 3. What is Special About UX Design

### Time-Based Nature
- Interactive systems are **time-based** — experiences unfold over time.
- Built on **action-response rules**: what users do, and how the system responds.
- Designing actions = defining what **commands/options** users have.
- Designing responses = deciding what **information** is presented back.
- Key usability focus: helping users **navigate complex systems** to accomplish their goals.

### Context is Critical
Context shapes everything in UX design. Key dimensions of context:

- **Who, how, when, and why**: for example, a student at a deadline vs. an admin during office hours using the same LMS portal — completely different needs and behaviors.
- **Other systems and devices**: e.g., booking a seat for Eid involves multiple devices and services.
- **Other activities happening at the same time**: divided attention scenarios, e.g., using FoodPanda while doing something else, or doctors using apps mid-consultation.
- **Other people involved**: e.g., a banking app may be used in the presence of others.
- **Actual user vs. user on paper**: the assumed user persona and the real user in the real world are often different.

## 4. The UX Design Process

The UX design process maps the generic design stages to specific UX activities:

| Generic Design Stage | UX Activity |
|---|---|
| Understand the problem | Study users: tasks, contexts, personas |
| Generate possible solutions | Sketch, storyboard, wireframes |
| Analyze and select | Apply UX criteria |
| Embody solutions | Build prototype |
| Assess (find new problems) | Apply UX research methods |
| ...and Repeat | ...and Repeat |

## 5. Prototypes

### Why Do We Prototype?

- Turn abstract ideas into something concrete that can actually be tested.
- Step back and check if the design is meeting goals or going off track.
- Give stakeholders something tangible to react to, so everyone is on the same page.
- Used in user testing to see if the design actually works for real users.

### Prototypes Can Have Many Forms

A "representation of design" can mean any of the following:

- Verbal description
- Sketch
- Storyboard
- Navigation / Flow diagram
- Physical model
- Video
- Formal specification

### In UX...

In UX, a prototype is a **representation of the system as it will be experienced by end users**.

There are three levels of fidelity:

- **Low Fidelity (Lo-Fi)**
- **Middle Fidelity (Mid-Fi)**
- **High Fidelity (Hi-Fi)**

---

## 6. Progression of Prototypes

Prototypes progress in both **fidelity** and **cost** over **time**:

- Lo-Fi prototype: low investment ($)
- Mid-Fi prototype: moderate investment ($$)
- Hi-Fi prototype: higher investment ($$$)

As fidelity increases, so does the cost and time investment. The progression moves from rough sketches to polished, realistic representations.

---

## 7. Lo-Fi Prototypes

### Address:
- Functionality
- Basic organization
- Task flow and coverage

### Ignore:
- Graphics, fonts, colors
- Programming
- Real data

Lo-Fi prototypes are about getting core functionality and flow right before investing in visuals or implementation.

---

## 8. Mid-Fi Prototypes

### Address:
- **Layout**: how specifically are all the different fields and controls going to be laid out?
- **Interactivity**: how does it actually feel to interact with the system?
- **Navigation**: how do you navigate from one part of the system to another?

### Ignore:
- Graphics, fonts, colors
- Programming
- Real data

---

## 9. Hi-Fi Prototypes

### Address:
- Graphic design
- Interaction details
- Realistic data

### Ignore:
- Backend programming
- Complete functional coverage
  - Hi-Fi prototypes are focused on the specific task that particular prototype is supposed to accomplish, not on building everything.

---

## 10. Avoid Premature Commitment

**Definition**: Investing resources (time and money) in design ideas that turn out to be a dead end.

### Problems with Premature Commitment:
- Wastes money and effort.
- Exhausts project resources; the team runs out of time.
- May result in being **stuck with a bad design** or one that is not optimal.
- It is **very difficult to let go** of the cognitive and emotional commitments that stakeholders make once they have invested significant time and resources into a particular design direction.

> **"Low fidelity prototypes are not precious and therefore invite more honest critique."**
> — Bill Moggridge

This is why starting with Lo-Fi is valuable: low investment means lower emotional attachment, which enables more open and honest feedback.

## 11. How Does Prototyping Fit Into the UX Design Process?

### What Happens Before Prototyping?

**Phase 1: Assess**
- Assessing current needs.
- Understanding the design problem.
- Establishing guidelines and constraints — what a good solution would look like.
- Producing **personas, scenarios, user stories, requirements** to guide the design process.

**Phase 2: Design**
- Ideation
- Sketching
- Selection
- Synthesis

### What Happens After Prototyping?

**Phase: Assess (again)**
- Assess whether you are on the right track.
- Conduct user testing, or apply inspection methods.
- Gather feedback from different stakeholders.
- Then: **Redesign** and **More Prototyping**.

The cycle is: Assess → Design → Build → Assess → Design → Build → ...

## 12. Sketching

### What is a Sketch?

A sketch is a **cheap, rapidly constructed representation of an idea**.

Key properties of a sketch:
- Quick
- Timely
- Inexpensive
- Disposable
- Plentiful
- Minimal detail
- Allows ambiguity

### Why Sketch?

Sketching helps you: **Create, Record, Reflect, Share, Critique, Decide.**

#### Create
- Early ideation.
- Think openly about ideas and think through them.
- Force yourself to visualize how things come together.
- Brainstorming: generate abundant ideas without worrying about quality.
- Invent and explore concepts.

#### Record
- Capture ideas you develop and come across.
- Archive ideas for later reflection.

#### Reflect, Share, Critique, Decide
- Communicate ideas to others.
- Invite responses, criticisms, and alternatives.
- Choose ideas worth pursuing.

### How to Sketch?

- Use pencil / paper / whiteboard.
- Go fast.
- Do not try to make it perfect.
- Make lots of them.
- You do **not** have to be "good at drawing."

### What to Sketch?

- **The Problem**: How would someone experience this problem?
- **The Solution**: What would it look like for the problem to be solved?

## 13. Getting the Right Design

### The Recommended Approach:
1. Generate many ideas and variations.
2. Reflect on all ideas.
3. Choose the ones that look most promising.
4. Develop them in parallel.
5. Add new ideas as they come up.
6. Then iterate on your final choice.

### The Problem: Fixation on the First Idea

If you generate a single idea and iterate only on that, you may be doing **local hill climbing** — improving one idea without ever knowing if a fundamentally better idea exists elsewhere in the design space.

- **Local maxima**: The best version of your current idea.
- **Global maxima**: The best possible design overall, which may require abandoning your first idea entirely.

The risk: you optimize idea 1 without ever discovering that idea 5 was a far superior starting point.

## 14. Opposing Processes in Design

Design involves two opposing forces that must be balanced throughout the process:

| Generation | Convergence |
|---|---|
| Creation of alternatives | Reduction of alternatives |
| Elaboration of ideas | Selection of contenders |

The design process starts at a **starting point**, expands outward through generation, then narrows back to an **endpoint** through convergence.

*(Figure adapted from Buxton, B. Sketching the User Experience)*

---

## 15. Elaboration and Reduction

These two processes alternate repeatedly throughout the design cycle:

- **Elaborate**: Generate solutions. These represent opportunities.
- **Reduce**: Decide on the ones worth pursuing (decision-making).
- **Repeat**: Elaborate and reduce again on those remaining solutions.

The design process is not a straight line but a series of expansions and contractions, alternating between opportunity-seeking and decision-making.

*(Source: Laseau, P. (1980) Graphic Thinking for Architects & Designers. John Wiley and Sons)*

## 16. A Sketch is Not a Prototype

The difference between a sketch and a prototype is:

- Always: **a contrast of purpose**
- Usually (but not always): **a contrast in form**

However, it is not a binary distinction — **it is a continuum**:

```
sketch ────────────────────────────────► prototype
```

As you move along the continuum, the artifact transitions from being evocative and exploratory to being specific and testable.

### Sketch vs. Prototype Comparison Table

| Sketch | Prototype |
|---|---|
| Evocative | Didactic |
| Suggest | Describe |
| Explore | Refine |
| Question | Answer |
| Propose | Test |
| Provoke | Resolve |
| Tentative | Specific |
| Noncommittal | Depiction |

---

## 17. From Sketches to Prototypes

- **Sketches**: Used during the early ideation stages of design. Low investment, focused on design exploration.
- **Prototypes**: Used for capturing and detailing the actual design. Higher investment, focused on usability and testing.

As time increases, investment increases. The evaluation criteria also shift:
- Early on: criteria weight is on **design** (are we exploring the right ideas?).
- Later: criteria weight shifts toward **usability** (does it work for users?).

*(Image from Bill Buxton's Book: Sketching User Experiences, 2007, Morgan Kaufmann)*

---

## 18. Sketching is About Design

- Sketching is **not about drawing**.
- It is **about design**.

### Sketching is a tool to help you:
- Express ideas.
- Develop ideas.
- Communicate design ideas.

### Sketching is part of a process:
- Idea generation
- Design elaboration
- Design choices
- Engineering

The key insight: artistic skill is irrelevant. What matters is the thinking and communication that sketching enables.
