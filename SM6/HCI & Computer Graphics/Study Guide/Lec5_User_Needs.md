## Understanding User Needs

### Trying to Understand What Users Want

Using the vending machine as a running example, key questions include:

- Do users want anything else with their soda?
- What methods of payment do users prefer?
- What do users want when a machine fails?

---

## Why Do a User Needs Assessment?

- To understand **why** people **want** to use a product or service
- To learn **how** people actually **use** or do not use a product or service
- To see **what** people **like or dislike** about a product or service
- To hear **what else** people might **want** from a product or service

---

## When to Do a User Needs Assessment?

### Before any design phase
- Before designing something new
- Before a re-design

### Anytime questions or problems arise
- Why would users want to use this service?
- What do they think about each time they use it?
- Why don't customers go beyond the home page of a particular website?
- What frustrates users of a given project?
- How could we improve the client's experience?

---

## "Needs" Are Not Always Urgent

- **Origin of Need Assessment:** 1960, Roger Kaufman, an educational technology researcher
- In UX, "needs" = needs, wants, preferences, quirks
  - Often focused on a product or a service
  - Pros and cons to taking a broader view

## Overview of Qualitative Research

### Qualitative Research Methodologies

**Data gathering:**
- Interviews (M1/M2)
- Field observations (M1/M2)
- Participant observations
- Focus groups
- Etc.

**Data analysis:**
- Coding
- Content analysis
- Thematic analysis (Via Affinity Diagrams — M1/M2)
- Recursive abstraction (Via Affinity Diagrams — M1/M2)

### Qualitative Research Caveats

Qualitative research is **not** the best for questions such as:
- What proportion of our users do X?
- Would usage increase if we did Y instead of Z?
- Does program V cause effect W?

**Weaknesses of qualitative research:**
- Providing numerical data or analysis
- Confirming hunches, guesses, intuitions
- Making close comparisons

## Quantitative Research (Sales Data? Survey? Usage Stats?)

### Quantitative research helps to understand...
- Overall trends
- Existing product or service
- Issues you can anticipate

### Quantitative research is less useful for...
- Detailed understanding
- New possibilities
- Unanticipated issues

### More Interesting Questions (GPS Redesign Example)

**Detailed Understanding:**
- How do users input a destination?
- Do they make multiple detours through different methodologies because they get stuck?
- Are they really frustrated at any phase?

**New Feature:**
- Quizzes to help people memorise routes

**Unanticipated Issue:**
- Might families want a "most fun" route?

## Semi-Structured Interviews

### Types of Interviews

- Group vs. **individual** (M1/M2)
- Remote vs. **in-person** (M1/M2)
- Degree of structure:
  - **Structured** — Questionnaires/Surveys
  - **Semi-structured** (M1/M2)
  - Unstructured

### Semi-Structured Interviews: Key Points

- Balance between researcher interests/focus and open, free-form discussion
- **Script, or interview protocol:** a list of questions used as a guideline
  - Won't actually follow them strictly
- Conversational style
- Expect to go off-script

### Interviews for Needs Assessment

**Who:**
- User / potential user of product or service
- "Typical" member of group you are interested in

**Where:**
- Where participant normally uses product or service (or might do so)
- Where participant feels comfortable

**When:**
- Scheduled in advance
- Mutually convenient time
- When participant normally uses product or service (or might do so)

---

## Components of an Interview Protocol

*(One set for every type of interview participant)*

1. Overarching question
2. Introduction
3. Main interview
   - Background / warm-up questions
   - Core questions
4. Observation
   - Think-aloud protocol
   - Observation questions
5. Conclusion

---

### Overarching Question

- This is the **big question** you are trying to answer
- Keep it in mind **throughout** protocol development and actual interview
- You **will not** necessarily ask the interview participant this question **directly**

---

### Introduction

Script for you to explain the process to the interview participant:

- Self-introduction
- Goals of interview
- Duration
- Overview of components
- Confidentiality
- Voluntary nature
- Permissions

---

### Main Interview

**Background / warm-up questions:**
- Short, Simple
- Closed-ended

**Core questions:**
- Open-ended
- Conversational
- Focused on overarching question

---

### Observation

**Think-aloud protocol:**
- Encourage participant to verbally explain what they are thinking and doing
- Awkward at first; gets easier

**Observation questions:**
- Prompts to perform tasks
- Questions you can anticipate
- Focused on overarching question

---

### Conclusion

Script for the end of the interview and observation:

- "Anything to add?"
- Thank you
- Follow-up plans:
  - Additional questions
  - Report back
- Your contact information
- "Any questions?"
- Thank you, again

---

### Additional Comments

- Guidelines only — no hard rules
- **Most important:** Interview ethics
- **Second most important:** Answering your overarching question

**Variations:**
- Multiple overarching questions
- Interleaving interview and observation
- No observation
- In-situ observation, with no think-aloud protocol

## Good Practices for Interview Protocols

### Interviewing as Science and Art

**Preparation (Science):**
- Interview participant background
- Topic background
- Interview protocol
- Overarching question

**Improvisation (Art):**
- Rapport with interview participant
- Attention to the unexpected
- Unscripted questions
- Focus on overarching question

---

### Good vs Not So Good Questions

#### Open-ended (Good)
- "Why did you...?"
- "How did you go about...?"
- "Can you tell me about...?"
- "Why do you think that is?"
- "How did you handle that situation?"

#### Closed-ended (Not So Good)
- "Do you like X?"
- "How many times a week do you...?"
- "What is your favorite...?"

*Note: Closed-ended questions are sometimes appropriate, but they should not dominate the protocol.*

---

#### Specific, Concrete (Good)
- "Tell me about the last time that you did..."
- "Can you think of a specific instance when...?"

#### Abstract, Generalized (Not So Good)
- "What do you normally do when...?"
- "What is the typical way in which...?"

*Note: These are not bad questions, but you want to be sure that you have asked for concrete details as well.*

---

#### Non-leading, Judgment-free (Good)
- "What option do you prefer?"
- "What do you think was the reason for that?"
- "How did you try to address the problem?"

#### Leading, Judgmental (Not So Good)
- "So, you prefer X over Y?"
- "Was it because of a bad process that the task was difficult?"
- "Why didn't you use feature Z?"

---

### Organization and Flow

- Cluster related questions together
- Within each cluster, arrange them in a meaningful order:
  - Chronological
  - Narrative
  - Broad to narrow, or narrow to broad
  - Etc.
- Arrange clusters in a meaningful order
- Save any sensitive questions for the end

---

### Follow-Up Questions

- Under each core question, add follow-up questions
- Use bulleted lists
- Follow-up question types:
  - **Generic:** "Why?" / "How did that happen?" / "Tell me more."
  - **Prompts for specific issues:** "Did you think about X?" / "Or Y?"
  - **Repetition:** "Could you tell me about another time when...?"
  - Etc.
- Think through the order of these as well
- You will probably not ask all of the follow-up questions

---

### Final Thoughts on Interview Protocol

- **Goals of protocol:** Preparation and guide during interview
- Memorize the overall flow of the protocol
- Remember: you will not read protocol questions one after the other
- See sample and guidelines: see GCR, interview template is provided under assessment milestone 1

## Tips for Interviewing

### Rapport with Interview Participant

- Make them feel comfortable
- Do not jump right in
- Start with small talk:
  - Finding something in common
  - Complimenting
  - Discussing the weather
- Adopt a learning mindset — they are the expert
- Observe, Adapt, **Listen, Listen, Listen!**

---

### Follow Interesting Threads

- Advanced preparation pays off
- Let go, but keep overarching question in mind — use protocol as back-up
- Ask follow-up questions until satisfied
- Listen for unexpected but relevant things
- Improvise unscripted follow-up questions
- Be curious!

---

### Note Taking

- Try using paper and pen/pencil — devices can create a mental barrier
- Print out interview protocol with blank space under core questions
- Write down:
  - Key points, in short phrases
  - Things that audio recording would not capture: impressions, facial expressions, physical context
  - Follow-up questions
  - Etc.
- Important, even if audio recording
- No "right" way to take notes — develop your own style

## Observing Users

### Observation Tips [1/2]

- Ask participant to perform relevant tasks
- **Think-aloud protocol:**
  - Ask participant to speak aloud and tell you what they are doing and thinking
  - It will be awkward at first, but encourage them by asking lots of quick questions:
    - "What are you doing now?"
    - "What are you thinking when you do that?"
    - Etc.

### Observation Tips [2/2]

- Take good notes
- Pay special attention to things they do that are not part of the main task:
  - Pauses
  - Missteps / multiple attempts
  - Intentional detours, Etc.
- Ask questions if you do not understand the motivation for an action

### Otherwise, Similar to Interviews

- Maintain conversational tone
- Keep overarching question in mind
- Be curious!
- Practice, practice, practice

### Observation Starts Even Before the Interview

- The physical context tells you a lot before the interview even begins
- Observe the environment, workspace, surroundings for clues about the user

## Extracting Data from the Interviews

### Data Extraction

**Raw data:**
- Notes
- Audio Recording

- Make some sense of the raw data — this process is called **data extraction**

---

### From Notes to Qualitative Data

- As soon as possible after an interview, review notes and audio recordings and write down **affinity notes**

**An affinity note is...**
- A sticky note on which you write:
  - A participant code (type and number: C01, C13, G04, M02, etc.)
  - A statement or question
  - That comes from your notes or audio recordings
  - Which is probably relevant to the needs assessment

---

### Affinity Note Guidelines [1/2]

**Include:**
- Factual statements — complete sentences
- Participant quotations (in quotation marks)
- Observations, as statements
- Interpretations of the above
- Questions

**Tips:**
- Make each note understandable on its own
- Be concrete but concise
- Aim for about one affinity note per minute of interview

---

### Affinity Note Guidelines [2/2]

**Interpretations:**
- Repeating themes
- Contradictions or conflicts
- Key findings
- Root issues
- Anything else that might be relevant

**Good quotes:**
- Unexpected
- Representative

## Analysing Qualitative Data

### The Secret to Intelligence

- The world is full of information — you cannot remember it all
- By clustering, sorting, finding patterns, and relating things to each other, you can condense information
- **Condensing is understanding**
- **Understanding is intelligence!**

---

## Affinity Walls

Affinity walls are a way to do qualitative data analysis.

### Affinity Wall Guidelines

**Building the wall:**
- At first, make clusters quickly
- Do not get too attached to clusters
- Move notes around as you see new patterns
- Re-consider clusters
- Aim for clusters of 3-7 notes
  - Break up if more than 7 notes
  - Merge if less than 3 notes
  - *Not a hard rule!*

**Writing next-level notes (the crux):**
- Summarize the knowledge in the cluster
- Summary should be a full sentence and make sense on its own
- Balance abstraction and precision
- Think!
- Stop creating levels when summaries become uninformative

## Conclusion

### Key Takeaways

- No rocket science — rules are simple
- Involves a lot of practice so that you can really get into the groove of how to put these ideas into practice

### Full Process Summary

1. Conduct a **User Needs Assessment** before any design phase
2. Use **qualitative research methods** — interviews, observations, affinity walls
3. Plan your **interview protocol** with: overarching question, introduction, warm-up questions, core questions, observation, conclusion
4. During the interview: build rapport, follow interesting threads, listen actively, take good notes
5. After the interview: write **affinity notes** as soon as possible
6. Analyse data using an **affinity wall** — cluster, sort, find patterns, summarize up to higher-level insights
7. Supplement with **quantitative research** (surveys, usage stats) for confirming trends and proportions
