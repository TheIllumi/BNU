# Usability Testing
### How to Know if Your Design Actually Works
**Evaluating User Experience**

> "The best designs are discovered through watching real users — not imagined in isolation."

---

## What is Usability Testing?

> "Usability testing is a technique to evaluate a product by testing it with representative users."

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Real Users | Actual target users -- not designers, not developers, not friends. |
| Real Tasks | Users perform realistic tasks with the actual product or prototype. |
| Observation | Researchers observe behavior, measure performance, and identify problems. |
| Iterative Process | Testing happens early and often -- findings drive redesign. |

---

## Why Does Usability Testing Matter?

| Statistic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 85% | of usability problems found with just 5 users |
| $1 | spent on UX returns $100 in value |
| 70% | of online businesses fail due to poor usability |

**Key Benefits:**
- Reveals real problems users face -- not assumed ones
- Validates design decisions with evidence
- Saves money by catching issues before development
- Increases user satisfaction and product adoption
- Provides data to prioritize design improvements

**Jakob Nielsen's Rule:**

> "Testing with 5 users will reveal approximately 85% of usability problems. You don't need more participants -- you need more test rounds."
>
> -- Jakob Nielsen, Nielsen Norman Group

## Types of Usability Testing

### Formative Testing
Done **DURING** design -- to find and fix problems.

- Small groups (3--5 users)
- Qualitative insights
- Iterative and frequent
- Low-fi or hi-fi prototypes
- Goal: Improve the design

### Summative Testing
Done **AFTER** design -- to measure performance.

- Larger groups (20+ users)
- Quantitative metrics
- Done at key milestones
- Working product
- Goal: Benchmark the design

**Also categorized by:** Moderated vs Unmoderated | In-person vs Remote | Explorative vs Assessment

---

## Moderated vs Unmoderated Testing

| Aspect | Moderated | Unmoderated |
|---|---|---|
| Facilitator | Present and active | No facilitator |
| Location | Lab or shared space | User's own environment |
| Probing | Can ask follow-up questions | No real-time clarification |
| Cost | Higher | Lower |
| Sample Size | Small (5--10) | Large (50--100+) |
| Data Type | Rich qualitative | Behavioral/quantitative |
| Best For | Complex tasks, early stages | Confirmatory testing |

## Planning a Usability Test

**Step 1 -- Define Goals**
What questions must the test answer?
Example: Can users complete checkout in less than 2 minutes?

**Step 2 -- Recruit Participants**
5--8 representative users from target audience. Match your persona characteristics.

**Step 3 -- Create Task Scenarios**
Realistic tasks -- not instructions.
Example: Find a blue shirt under $50 -- not: Click Shop.

**Step 4 -- Choose Method and Tools**
Moderated/unmoderated, in-person/remote.
Tools: Maze, UserTesting, Lookback, paper.

**Step 5 -- Prepare the Protocol**
Script, consent forms, equipment checks, pilot test with one person first.

**Step 6 -- Run and Observe**
Stay neutral -- observe, don't help. Record everything: time, errors, expressions.

---

## Writing Good Task Scenarios

**Rules for Writing Tasks:**
- Use realistic scenarios, not instructions
- Don't give away the answer in the task
- Avoid technical jargon
- Include a clear end condition
- Make it specific enough to be actionable
- Test the tasks yourself first

**Bad vs Good Examples:**

| Bad | Good |
|---|---|
| Click on the Shop button | You want to buy a birthday gift for your sister. Find something under Rs. 2000. |
| Go to your profile settings | You moved to a new city. Update your delivery address. |
| Use the search function | You're looking for a laptop with 16GB RAM. Try to find one. |

## Running the Test -- Roles and Protocol

**Facilitator**
Guides the session. Gives tasks, stays neutral, does NOT help. Asks "What are you thinking?" -- never "Click here."

**Note-taker**
Documents observations -- where users hesitate, errors made, expressions, verbal comments. Separate from facilitator.

**Participant (User)**
The real user. Performs tasks, thinks aloud. They are NOT being tested -- the DESIGN is being tested.

**Observers**
Team members watch silently (separate room or via video). Must not interrupt. Can note questions for post-session debrief.

> **Golden Rule: The user is never wrong. The design is wrong.**

---

## The Think-Aloud Protocol

Users verbalize their thoughts, feelings, and decisions AS they perform tasks. This is the most widely used method in usability testing.

**How to Conduct:**
- Introduce: "Please think out loud as you work -- say whatever comes to mind."
- Do not interpret silence -- prompt gently: "What are you thinking right now?"
- Never answer questions about the interface during the task
- Let users struggle -- note where and how long they struggle
- After task: ask retrospective questions

**What You Discover:**
- Where users feel confused or lost
- What mental model users have of the system
- What labels or words users use vs what the UI uses
- Emotional reactions (frustration, delight, surprise)
- Why users make errors -- not just that they made them

## Measuring Usability -- Key Metrics

### The PULSE Framework -- Core Usability Metrics

| Letter | Dimension | What It Measures |
|---|---|---|
| P | Performance | Task completion rate, time-on-task, error rate |
| U | Usage | How often, how long, which features |
| L | Learnability | Time to first success, help-seeking behavior |
| S | Satisfaction | SUS score, NPS, post-test questionnaire |
| E | Errors | Error frequency, severity, recovery time |

**SUS (System Usability Scale):** 10-question questionnaire, scored 0--100. Above 68 = above average usability.

## Analyzing and Reporting Results

### Nielsen's Severity Rating Scale for Usability Problems

| Rating | Label | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Not a Usability Problem | Cosmetic only -- fix if time allows |
| 1 | Cosmetic Problem | Minor, can be fixed with low priority |
| 2 | Minor Usability Problem | Low priority but should be fixed in next release |
| 3 | Major Usability Problem | Important -- high priority to fix |
| 4 | Usability Catastrophe | Imperative to fix BEFORE product launch |

**Analysis Steps:**
1. Compile all observations
2. Identify recurring issues
3. Rate severity
4. Prioritize fixes
5. Write recommendations

## Real-World Usability Test Examples

### Amazon
- **Problem:** Users abandoned the checkout when forced to create an account.
- **Finding:** 72% of users chose "Create Account" by default, leading to frustration.
- **Fix:** Added "Guest Checkout" option. Result: +$300 million revenue in first year.

### Airbnb
- **Problem:** Users were not booking despite landing on listing pages.
- **Finding:** Think-aloud revealed users didn't trust low-quality listing photos.
- **Fix:** Hired professional photographers for all listings. Bookings increased by 2x.

### NHS UK
- **Problem:** Patients couldn't find how to book GP appointments online.
- **Finding:** Users looked for "Book Appointment" but button was labeled "Contact Us."
- **Fix:** Renamed the button. Task success rate jumped from 32% to 89%.

## Common Mistakes in Usability Testing

**Testing the User, Not the Design**
Always remind participants: "We are testing the design, not you. There are no wrong answers."

**Helping the User During Tasks**
Stay silent when users struggle. Silence is data. Helping invalidates results.

**Testing with Team Members**
Use real target users. Designers and developers know the system too well -- they find it easy.

**Too Many Tasks**
Keep sessions to 60--90 minutes max. 5--8 tasks. Quality over quantity.

**No Clear Goals**
Know exactly what questions you need answered before recruiting any participants.

**Not Documenting Properly**
Record sessions with consent. Take detailed notes. You'll miss 50% of insights from memory alone.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Test Early, Test Often** -- Usability testing at every stage saves time and money.
2. **5 Users Reveal 85%** -- Small tests are powerful -- quantity does not equal quality.
3. **The User is Never Wrong** -- If users fail, the design failed. Not the user.
4. **Think Aloud = Gold** -- Verbal narration reveals the WHY behind behavior.
5. **Prioritize with Severity** -- Not all problems are equal -- fix catastrophes first.
6. **Document Everything** -- If you didn't write it down, it didn't happen.

## Practice Questions

### Question 1

A startup building a food delivery app for Lahore has a limited budget of Rs. 50,000 for usability testing. Their prototype is a Figma clickable mockup. Their lead designer says: "We should just test with our own team -- we all use food delivery apps regularly." Their product manager disagrees and wants to hire a usability testing agency.

**(a)** Is the designer's suggestion acceptable? Give a specific reason why or why not. *(2 marks)*

**(b)** Given the Rs. 50,000 budget constraint, propose a realistic, effective testing approach. Be specific about participant count, method, and what tools or materials are needed. *(3 marks)*

### Question 2

A team tested a hospital appointment booking system with 10 users. The following data was collected:

- **Task 1 (Book an appointment):** 7/10 users completed successfully. Average time: 4 min 20 sec. Industry benchmark: 2 min.
- **Task 2 (Cancel an appointment):** 9/10 users completed. Average time: 1 min 10 sec.
- **Task 3 (Find doctor specialization):** 4/10 users completed successfully. 6 users either gave up or found incorrect information.

**(a)** Calculate the task completion rate for each task and identify which task has the most critical usability problem. Justify your answer. *(3 marks)*

**(b)** Task 1 has a 70% completion rate but its average time is more than double the industry benchmark (4:20 vs 2:00). What does this combination of metrics tell you about the design? *(2 marks)*
